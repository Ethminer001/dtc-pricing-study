Public-data teardown · 71 storefronts · 22,956 products

Advice says price everything at $X.99.
12 of the 71 stores in this study do.

I pulled the public catalogue of 71 Shopify brands and measured their price structure: entry points, ladder spread, compare-at discounts, bundles, variant depth and availability. Most stores charm-price almost nothing. Compare-at prices sit on a minority of products. Most products in most catalogues are in stock.

Analysis date: 24 October 2026  ·  Source: public /products.json endpoints  ·  Scripts available on request
Why I ran this

Most operators set prices by copying the last brand they saw or a tip from TikTok. I measured what 71 brands do instead.

This study covers price structure. It does not cover price performance. Every number comes from public catalogue data, and the methodology section at the bottom shows how to reproduce or challenge each one.

Finding 01 · price endings

Round prices outnumber charm prices 3 to 1.

20% of the 22,956 products end in .99 or a 9. 60% end in a round number. The most common price points are $45, $38, $35, $40 and $30.

How each sampled product price ends. Round = whole-dollar prices not ending in 9, including round-5. Percentages are of all products; the 2% priced under $10 have no bar.

Stores pick a style and keep it. 48 stores charm-price under 20% of their catalogue, 12 charm-price half or more, and few sit between.

Distribution of 71 stores by share of their catalogue priced .99 or ending in 9.

So what? Within a store, price endings tend to follow one house style. This study cannot say which style sells better. A catalogue that mixes endings is unusual in this cohort.

Finding 02 · discounting

Compare-at prices are common, and most stores use them on a minority of products.

A popular claim says DTC brands run inflated compare-at prices across the whole catalogue. 63 of 71 stores use compare-at pricing somewhere. Among those 63, the median store carries one on 19% of its products, and 6% of all stores carry one on 80% or more.

Where a compare-at price exists, the typical discount is 25%. Half of the stores that use them sit between 15% and 33%, and the 10th to 90th percentile runs from about 10% to 46%.

So what? This measures whether a compare-at price is present. It cannot show whether the "was" price ever sold. Check whether your own catalogue applies one discount depth to a $12 item and a $200 item.

Finding 03 · availability

Most products are buyable. About one store in seven has a real gap.

I checked each product for at least one buyable variant on the snapshot day. The median store has 93% of its products available. 31 of 71 stores sit at 95% or above and 10 sit below 75%.

Distribution of stores by share of products with at least one buyable variant.

My first pass counted variants. That count tracked the number of sizes a store sells: a shoe brand with 12 sizes shows more gaps than a one-size coffee brand (correlation with variant depth: −0.39). I switched to products, which removes the effect.

What this does not show

This is a one-day snapshot, and a sold-out product can be deliberate, such as a limited drop or a seasonal line. Public data cannot show whether sold-out products receive paid traffic. Answering that takes Shopify inventory joined to ad-platform data, and you can run that check on your own store.

Finding 04 · a structural checklist

Three checks are near-universal. Four split the stores.

I scored each store against seven structural checks. The criteria are my own, not an industry standard, and they describe a catalogue without grading it. A majority-charm catalogue has a style, which carries no merit of its own.

Share of the 71 stores passing each check.

A sub-$25 entry product, a price ladder of 3× or more, and a clean taxonomy appear in most stores, so passing them says little. The other four vary much more.

Stores by number of the seven checks passed. Median score: 4/7. 1 store passes all seven, and 9 score 6 or more.

Exploratory · read with caution

Stores with bundles tend to carry fewer variants.

The 41 stores with a bundle or multi-pack programme have a median of 1 variants per product. The 11 stores with almost no bundles have a median of 7.

Median variants per product. Bundle stores: ≥10% of products flagged as bundles. No-bundle stores: <2%.

I read no strategy into this. The bundle flag comes from product titles (pack, set, kit, refill), the comparison group has 11 stores, and category may explain the gap: consumables get bundled and apparel gets sizes. Check your own catalogue instead of treating this as evidence.

What this study can and cannot tell you

This study has six limits:

  • It measures structure, not outcomes. Public catalogue data holds no revenue, traffic, conversion rate or ad spend, so nothing here shows that a pattern causes sales. The test of cause is an A/B test on your own store.
  • Survivorship. The sample holds brands still trading and still findable. Brands that closed may have used the same pricing.
  • The sample is not random. I selected 71 English-language, US-facing Shopify brands from well-known DTC names, including large ones (Allbirds, Everlane and Fashion Nova). Small stores may differ. The study covers Shopify only, with no Amazon or marketplace brands.
  • Snapshots drift. I pulled the data on 24 October 2026. Prices, stock and catalogues change weekly, so rerunning the scripts gives different numbers.
  • Some definitions are mine. The seven checks in Finding 04 are my criteria. Bundles come from title keywords, which flag some false positives and miss some real bundles.
  • Three currencies are unconfirmed. Three of the 71 stores did not expose a currency. I treat them as USD because they are US-facing brands.

This report publishes no store-level scores or rankings.

Ten minutes

Three checks for your own store

  1. Price endings. Export your product prices and count the distinct endings. More than two suggests nobody chose a house style.
  2. Variants and bundles. Count your median variants per product and the share of your catalogue that is a bundle or multi-pack. If both are low, you have no tested way to raise order value.
  3. Stock and ad spend. List your out-of-stock variants and check which of those products had active campaigns last month. Each match is ad spend sent to a sold-out product.

Most brands cannot answer the third question, because Shopify and the ad platforms hold the data in separate places.

Methodology

How I built it

  • Data source. Every Shopify storefront publishes its catalogue at /products.json, the feed that powers its own product pages. The collection used no HTML scraping, no login and no API key. I ran 6 concurrent requests with throttling and a 1,000-product cap per store.
  • Sample. 73 candidate domains were probed, and all exposed a public catalogue. I excluded two storefronts (one CAD, one AUD) from price aggregates, which leaves 71 stores.
  • Product count. 22,956 products. Stores with more than 1,000 products contribute their first 1,000, so very large catalogues are sampled.
  • Price used. The lowest variant price per product, which is the entry price a shopper sees.
  • Availability. A product counts as in stock if at least one variant is available. I rejected a variant-level count because it tracks how many sizes a store sells.
  • Charm pricing. A price counts as charm if it is x.99, or a whole-dollar price ending in 9 (for example $29), at $10 or above.
  • Anchor depth. (compare_at − price) / compare_at, taken on products where a compare-at price exists and exceeds the sale price.
  • Currency. I checked each store through /cart.js or its product-page markup. 68 of the 71 stores are confirmed USD. Three exposed no currency to automated checks, and I include them as USD because they are US-facing brands. That is the one assumption in the study.
  • Dates. I harvested the data on 24 October 2026. The scripts (harvest.py, analyze.py) are available on request.
